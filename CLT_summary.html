CLT Project 1: Monte Carlo Investigation

An Empirical Evaluation of the Central Limit Theorem

Author

Adam Abramyan

1 1. Executive Summary: Is \(n = 30\) the Magic Number?

The primary goal of this Monte Carlo investigation is to evaluate when the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) works, when it works slowly, and when its assumptions break down. Based on the evidence gathered, \(n = 30\) is not a universal cutoff for normality.

The rate at which the sampling distribution becomes approximately normal depends heavily on the original population: * Symmetric distributions like the Standard Normal and Six-Sided Die become normal almost immediately. * Skewed or discrete distributions like the Exponential and Small Binomial retain structural character well into higher \(n\) values, with the Binomial not forming a standard distribution until after \(n = 30\) and the Exponential retaining a slight right skew even at \(n = 40\). * Heavy-tailed distributions like the Cauchy do not exhibit consistent adjustments toward normal at any sample size.

2 2. Methodology & Criteria

2.1 2.1 Distinguishing \(n\) from Monte Carlo Simulations

In this investigation, \(n\) represents the number of observations in a single sample, while the number of simulations represents how many samples are repeatedly generated to build the sampling distribution. To improve accuracy, the number of simulations was quadrupled from initial tests, and the \(n\) values evaluated were expanded from \(10\) to \(40\).

2.2 2.2 Definition of “Approximately Normal”

A distribution is considered “approximately normal” when: 1. The empirical histogram is reasonably bell-shaped and symmetric. 2. The Q-Q plot is approximately linear. 3. Skewness and kurtosis fall to a consistently low level. 4 5. Limitations and Surprises

Limitations: * A larger sample size and more Monte Carlo simulations are always useful, but the investigation was limited by compute. * Only one parameter value was checked for each type of distribution (e.g., a large lambda or a different binomial probability might perform differently). While most data was covered, useful parameters may have been missed.

Surprises: * I was surprised by how much the distributions retained their character even at higher \(n\) values. * The binomial distribution took much longer than expected, not forming a standard distribution until after \(n = 30\). * The exponential graphs retained a very slight right skew even at \(n = 40\).
